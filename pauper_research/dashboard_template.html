MTG Pauper tournament results. See what a deck beats, what beats it, and which cards move its win rate.

How to read this

  • Win rate is match wins excluding mirrors, with draws as half a win.
  • On every bar the centre line is 50% (or no difference). The whisker is the 95% interval: the range the true value most likely sits in.
  • Clear means the whole interval is on one side of the line. Lean means it could still be even. Headings such as "Good against" list only Clear results, in solid colour; Lean ones are outlined and folded under "Could go either way".
  • Share of lists counts published decklists, which are mostly MTGO's winning lists. Win rates count matches, which come mostly from paper events. The line under the title says how the current view splits.
  • Card effects compare lists of the same deck with and without a card. They show what winning lists play, which isn't always what makes them win. With dozens of cards, about 1 in 20 can look Clear by chance.
  • Online is MTGO: many lists, but match results only from Challenge top 8s. Paper is Melee, CardsRealm and Topdeck, with every round, mostly from Italy and Brazil.

How every number is calculated

The formulas behind each figure on this page, in the order you meet them. Values in bold are the settings this build used.

The short version

  • Win rate is the share of matches a deck wins against other decks, with draws as half a win.
  • Every figure has a 95% interval: the range the true value most likely sits in. Small samples give wide intervals.
  • Clear means the whole interval is on one side of 50% (or of no difference). Lean means it could still be even, so treat it as a hint, not a result.
  • Share of lists and win rate come from different data: published lists are mostly MTGO, match results mostly paper. See Data sources.
  • With pilot skill correction on, a deck doesn't get credit for being played by strong players.

    How much each source contributes

    Sources are not weighted: every decklist and every match counts the same, whichever site it comes from. So a source's influence on a statistic is its share of the data behind it:

    \[ c_s^{X} = \frac{n_s^{X}}{\sum_t n_t^{X}} \]

    \(n_s^{X}\): what source \(s\) adds to statistic \(X\). For meta share, decklists \(d_s\). For win rates, non-mirror matches from a known deck's side \(|M_s|\) (a match between two known, different decks counts once for each). For matchups, the same restricted to opponents with a published list. For the pilot model, the recency weight \(\sum_{m \in s} w_m\) of its matches (one side each) in the -day fit.

    Pooling is the same as a weighted average of the per-source figures, with these shares as weights. For deck \(a\):

    \[ \hat p_a = \sum_s \frac{n_{a,s}}{n_a}\, \hat p_{a,s}, \qquad \text{share}_a = \sum_s \frac{d_s}{\sum_t d_t}\, \text{share}_{a,s} \]

    \(n_{a,s}\), \(\hat p_{a,s}\): deck \(a\)'s non-mirror matches and win rate in source \(s\); \(\text{share}_{a,s}\): its meta share among that source's lists. Paper sites report every round, so they carry most of the win-rate and matchup weight. MTGO publishes many lists but only top-8 rounds, so it counts for more in meta share than in win rates.

    Match results

    Each match is scored from the games won \(g_w\), lost \(g_l\) and drawn:

    \[ s_m = \begin{cases} 1 & g_w > g_l \\ \tfrac12 & g_w = g_l \\ 0 & g_w < g_l \end{cases} \]

    Byes and matches with no games are dropped. Every match is stored once from each player's side, so its two scores add up to 1.

    Meta share

    \[ \text{share}_a = \frac{d_a}{\sum_b d_b} \]

    \(d_a\): published decklists of archetype \(a\) in the period. For MTGO this counts only the lists that are published (Challenge top 32, a sample of 5-0 League lists), so it is the share among winning decks, not of the whole field.

    Win rate

    \[ \hat p_a = \frac{W_a}{n_a} = \frac{1}{|M_a|} \sum_{m \in M_a} s_m \]

    \(M_a\): matches played by decks of archetype \(a\) against any other archetype. Mirrors are left out because they are always 50%. Opponents with no published list count here but not in any matchup. A matchup's win rate uses the same formula, restricted to matches against one opposing archetype.

    95% intervals (Wilson score)

    The whisker on every bar. With \(z = 1.959964\) (the 97.5th percentile of the normal distribution):

    \[ \begin{gathered} \tilde p = \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \\ h = \frac{z}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p (1 - \hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}} \end{gathered} \]
    \[ [L, U] = \big[\max(0,\ \tilde p - h),\ \min(1,\ \tilde p + h)\big] \]

    Unlike \(\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\), this stays inside 0–100% and behaves well at the small samples typical of matchups. With \(n = 30\) and \(\hat p = 0.5\) it is about ±17 points wide.

    Clear and Lean

    \[ \text{Clear} \iff L > c \ \text{ or } \ U < c \]

    Otherwise Lean. \(c = 0.5\) for win rates and matchups, \(c = 0\) for card differences. Clear means the whole interval is on one side of the line. With dozens of cards, about 1 in 20 can look Clear by chance.

    The labels decide how results are grouped. "Good against" and "Bad against" on a deck page, and "Lists with it win more" and "win less" for cards, hold only Clear results, drawn as solid bars. Lean results are drawn as outlined bars in a folded "Could go either way" group, which opens by itself when nothing in the section is Clear. Deck pages sort Clear matchups by how far the interval is from 50%: good ones by \(L\), bad ones by \(U\).

    In the reports, an archetype is flagged as skewed between online and paper when the two intervals don't overlap: \(L_\text{online} > U_\text{paper}\) or \(L_\text{paper} > U_\text{online}\).

    Card comparisons

    Within one archetype, decks playing card \(c\) (set \(D^+\)) are compared with decks that don't (\(D^-\)), pooling their non-mirror matches:

    \[ \hat p^{+} = \frac{W^{+}}{n^{+}}, \quad \hat p^{-} = \frac{W^{-}}{n^{-}}, \quad \Delta = \hat p^{+} - \hat p^{-} \]

    Its 95% interval uses Newcombe's hybrid score method, built from the two Wilson intervals \([L^\pm, U^\pm]\):

    \[ \begin{gathered} L_\Delta = \Delta - \sqrt{(\hat p^{+} - L^{+})^2 + (U^{-} - \hat p^{-})^2} \\ U_\Delta = \Delta + \sqrt{(U^{+} - \hat p^{+})^2 + (\hat p^{-} - L^{-})^2} \end{gathered} \]

    The same comparison per matchup counts only matches against one opponent. Play rate is \(|D^+| / (|D^+| + |D^-|)\). These are descriptive: lists with a card also differ in event, date and other cards.

    Pilot skill

    Model

    One regularized Bradley–Terry (logistic) model fits player ratings \(r\) and deck strengths \(\delta\) together, so neither gets credit for the other. For a match between players \(i\) and \(j\) on decks \(a\) and \(b\) in quarter \(q\):

    \[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(i \text{ beats } j) = \sigma(\eta), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \\ \eta = (r_i - r_j) + (\delta_{a,q} - \delta_{b,q}) \end{gathered} \]

    Decks without a published list get their own "Unknown" level. Players are matched by name, normalized (Unicode NFKC, whitespace collapsed, case folded) and merged across sources. Mirror matches are included, since there the deck terms cancel. Draws enter as \(s = \tfrac12\). Names never leave the model.

    Fit

    Matches from the days up to the end of the period, one fit per event scope, weighted by recency with a half-life of days:

    \[ w_m = 0.5^{\,\text{age}_m / h} \]

    \(\text{age}_m\): days between the match and the end of the period; \(h\) = days.

    \[ \begin{gathered} \min_{r,\,\delta}\ \sum_m w_m\, \mathcal{L}(\eta_m, s_m) \\ + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_i r_i^2 + \frac{\lambda_d}{2} \sum_{a,q} \delta_{a,q}^2 \\ \mathcal{L}(\eta, s) = \log\big(1 + e^{\eta}\big) - s\,\eta \end{gathered} \]

    \(\mathcal{L}\) is the log-loss of a prediction \(\sigma(\eta)\) for score \(s\). \(\lambda_d = \) is a weak prior on deck strengths that keeps thin deck–quarters from running off. \(\lambda\) pulls player ratings towards average; a player with few matches stays close to 0.

    \(\lambda\) is chosen per fit from \(\{\)\(\}\) by -fold cross-validation, holding out whole events, minimizing the weighted held-out log-loss, where \(\hat\eta_m^{(-k)}\) is predicted by the fit without fold \(F_k\):

    \[ \hat\lambda = \arg\min_{\lambda} \frac{\sum_{k} \sum_{m \in F_k} w_m\, \mathcal{L}\big(\hat\eta_m^{(-k)}, s_m\big)}{\sum_m w_m} \]

    The solver is Newton's method with a backtracking line search. Each step solves the sparse system with preconditioned conjugate gradients. \(X\) has one row per match with \(\pm 1\) in the players' and decks' columns, \(\theta = (r, \delta)\) and \(\Lambda\) holds the penalties:

    \[ \begin{gathered} g = X^\top W\big(\sigma(\eta) - s\big) + \Lambda\theta \\ H = X^\top \operatorname{diag}\!\big(w\,\sigma(\eta)(1 - \sigma(\eta))\big) X + \Lambda \\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - t\,H^{-1} g \end{gathered} \]

    Pilot lift and adjusted win rate

    Each match gets a lift: the win chance with both players' ratings minus the win chance with both ratings at 0.

    \[ \ell_m = \sigma(\eta_m) - \sigma\big(\eta_m - (r_i - r_j)\big) \]
    \[ \bar\ell = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{m \in M} \ell_m, \qquad \hat p^{\,\text{adj}} = \hat p - \bar\ell \]

    over exactly the matches \(M\) behind \(\hat p\), so only the pilots change and the opponents stay the same. Card comparisons subtract each side's lift: \(\Delta^{\text{adj}} = \Delta - (\bar\ell^{+} - \bar\ell^{-})\).

    Adjusted intervals

    The ratings are refitted on \(B = \) bootstrap samples. Each draws events with replacement, so event \(e\) appears \(c_e^{(b)}\) times and its matches get weight \(w_m c_{e(m)}^{(b)}\). \(\hat\lambda\) stays fixed. The spread of the lift across samples, \(s_\ell = \operatorname{sd}_b\big(\bar\ell^{(b)}\big)\) (for cards, of \(\bar\ell^{+(b)} - \bar\ell^{-(b)}\)), is added to each side of the Wilson or Newcombe interval:

    \[ \begin{gathered} L^{\text{adj}} = \hat p^{\,\text{adj}} - \sqrt{(\hat p - L)^2 + (z\,s_\ell)^2} \\ U^{\text{adj}} = \hat p^{\,\text{adj}} + \sqrt{(U - \hat p)^2 + (z\,s_\ell)^2} \end{gathered} \]

    Win-rate intervals are then clipped to [0, 1]. The adjustment is conservative: because ratings are shrunk towards average, only part of a pilot's edge is removed when they have few matches.

    Skill sensitivity

    The pilot-skill model assumes a rating edge is worth the same on every deck. Some decks reward skill more than others, so a second fit gives each archetype its own skill slope, over the same 12 months:

    \[ \begin{gathered} \Pr(i \text{ beats } j) = \sigma\big(o_m + \beta_a\, \tilde r_i - \beta_b\, \tilde r_j\big) \\ \beta_a = \beta_0 + \gamma_a \end{gathered} \]

    \(o_m = \delta_{a,q} - \delta_{b,q}\) is the deck part of the fitted model, held fixed. \(\tilde r_i\) is player \(i\)'s out-of-fold rating: it comes from the cross-validation fit that left out this match's event, so a rating is never built from the match it predicts. \(\beta_0\) is shared by all decks.

    \[ \min_{\beta_0,\,\gamma}\ \sum_m w_m\, \mathcal{L}(\eta_m, s_m) + \frac{\lambda_\gamma}{2} \sum_a \gamma_a^2 \]

    \(\lambda_\gamma = \) pulls every deck towards the shared slope, so decks with little data look average.

    A deck's sensitivity compares its slope with the average deck, weighting decks by matches \(n_a\) and leaving out "Unknown":

    \[ S_a = \frac{\beta_a}{\bar\beta}, \qquad \bar\beta = \frac{\sum_a n_a \beta_a}{\sum_a n_a} \]

    \(S_a = 1\) is average. \(S_a = 1.5\) means a rating edge moves this deck's win chance (on the logit scale) 1.5 times as much as on the average deck. The 95% interval comes from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of \(S_a\) over the bootstrap samples of events, with the ratings held fixed.

    The sentence on each deck page:

    • Very sensitive to player skill: the whole interval is above 1.
    • Less sensitive than most decks: the whole interval is below 1.
    • Seems somewhat (or a little less) sensitive: \(S_a \ge\) (or \(\le\) ), but the interval includes 1.
    • About as sensitive as an average deck: otherwise.

    Decks need + matches in the 12 months. This measures how much skill pays off once a deck is known, not how long it takes to learn.
